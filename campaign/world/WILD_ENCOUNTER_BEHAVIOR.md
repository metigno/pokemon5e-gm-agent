# WILD ENCOUNTER BEHAVIOR SYSTEM — D&D-STYLE WILDLIFE

## Purpose

Wild Pokémon are living creatures in Asteria, not videogame battle triggers.

A random encounter is a **scene involving wildlife**, and only becomes combat when the fiction, the Pokémon's motives, or the player's actions make combat happen.

The GM must treat wild Pokémon as animals/creatures with ecology, instincts, intelligence, social bonds, territory, fear, hunger, learned behavior and memory.

**Encounter != combat.**

A wild Pokémon may ignore the party, watch it, flee, hide, forage, play, hunt, defend young, protect territory, steal food, investigate humans, ask for help, cooperate, threaten, attack, retreat, pursue, or in extreme cases attempt to kill a human.

Use exact Pokémon 5e rules whenever the scene becomes mechanical.

---

## 1. Encounter scene pipeline

For every ordinary wild encounter:

1. Determine the exact location and valid species from the canonical ecology pool.
2. Apply time, weather, season/daypart, habitat access, ecological stress and world-state changes.
3. Select the species by the active rarity system.
4. Determine **social structure**.
5. Determine **current activity**.
6. Determine **disposition toward the party**.
7. Determine **stakes / protected interest**.
8. Determine **escalation threshold**.
9. Describe observable behavior before exposing mechanics.
10. Let the player choose what to do.
11. Resolve checks/combat/capture with Pokémon 5e only if needed.
12. Persist meaningful consequences.

Do not open with a videogame-style assumption such as "A wild X appeared — fight or capture?"

Present the situation first.

---

## 2. Social structure

Choose a structure that fits the species, environment and current fiction.

Possible structures:

- **solitary** — one independent individual;
- **pair** — mates, hunting pair, bonded companions or temporary partners;
- **family** — adults with young;
- **pack** — coordinated social group;
- **herd/flock/school** — larger mobile group;
- **colony** — many individuals sharing nesting/resource territory;
- **mixed group** — compatible species sharing protection, food or migration;
- **temporary aggregation** — unrelated individuals gathered around food, water, weather, migration or danger.

Do not force pack behavior onto every species.

A social group may have sentries, juveniles, injured members, hunters, caretakers or dominant individuals.

---

## 3. Alpha / Beta roles

Alpha and Beta are **ecological/social roles**, not automatic miniboss labels.

### Alpha

An Alpha can be:

- the dominant adult;
- an unusually mature or experienced individual;
- the principal defender;
- the group's decision-maker;
- a territorial apex individual;
- the individual others defer to.

An Alpha may be larger, older, more experienced or mechanically stronger when supported by the species and encounter fiction, but **Alpha does not automatically mean aggressive**.

A calm Alpha may avoid needless injury and simply warn intruders away.

### Beta

A Beta may be:

- second-ranked individual;
- sentry;
- principal hunter;
- subordinate defender;
- challenger to the Alpha;
- future dominant individual.

Not every group has an Alpha/Beta hierarchy.

Never assign Alpha/Beta purely because an encounter needs a harder fight.

Any mechanical Alpha/Beta modification must be supported by an existing legal Pokémon 5e rule or an explicit campaign rule. Narrative rank alone does not invent stat bonuses.

---

## 4. Current activity

A wild Pokémon should already be doing something before the player arrives.

Examples:

- foraging;
- grazing;
- drinking;
- sleeping;
- nesting;
- migrating;
- playing;
- grooming;
- guarding young;
- guarding food;
- hunting prey;
- stalking;
- feeding from a carcass;
- fighting a rival;
- escaping a predator;
- injured and hiding;
- trapped;
- displaced by weather/fire/humans;
- observing a settlement;
- scavenging human food;
- following another Pokémon;
- interacting with another species;
- seeking help.

The activity should create the scene's context and possible non-combat solutions.

---

## 5. Disposition toward humans

Disposition is not the same as alignment or permanent personality.

Possible starting dispositions include:

- **indifferent** — humans are irrelevant;
- **curious** — observes or approaches;
- **habituated** — accustomed to people;
- **cautious** — keeps distance and evaluates;
- **fearful** — prefers escape;
- **territorial** — wants the party gone;
- **protective** — guarding young, mate, nest, group or resource;
- **food-seeking** — interested in provisions;
- **predatory** — evaluating a creature as prey;
- **hostile** — already disposed to attack;
- **desperate** — pain, starvation, panic or displacement drives extreme behavior;
- **cooperative** — willing to work with humans for a current goal.

Disposition may change during play.

Species lore, prior local human behavior, ranger pressure, poaching, feeding, previous encounters and world events can all modify it.

---

## 6. Innocuous vs dangerous wildlife

A Pokémon can be harmless in one context and deadly in another.

The GM must distinguish:

- **innocuous presence** — no immediate threat;
- **potential hazard** — dangerous if approached/provoked;
- **active threat** — currently willing to attack;
- **lethal threat** — willing to use force that can realistically kill.

Power alone does not define intent.

A powerful Pokémon may ignore humans.
A weak frightened Pokémon may attack recklessly.
A parent may become dangerous only if the party approaches its young.

Do not make every carnivorous or predatory species automatically hunt humans.

---

## 7. Attacking or killing humans

There is no automatic narrative immunity for human trainers.

A wild Pokémon **can attempt to seriously injure or kill a human** when the fiction supports it, including situations such as:

- active predation;
- extreme territorial defense;
- defense of young or group;
- panic with no perceived escape;
- severe hunger;
- injury/rage;
- learned hostility caused by abuse or poaching;
- supernatural/anomalous influence;
- an apex encounter where humans are credible prey or threats.

However, lethal intent must have a cause.

Most wild creatures prefer survival over unnecessary injury. Many will disengage if:

- the threat retreats;
- territory is vacated;
- young/resource becomes safe;
- they are seriously hurt;
- they are intimidated;
- they obtain the food/object they wanted;
- a safer escape opens.

Once combat begins, use exact Pokémon 5e death, damage, condition, movement and action rules. Do not soften legal consequences solely because the target is a player character.

---

## 8. Warning and escalation

Before many attacks, wildlife may communicate through observable signals:

- posture;
- growls/calls;
- raised fur/feathers;
- elemental discharge;
- circling;
- mock charges;
- territorial marking;
- blocking a path;
- surrounding behavior;
- protecting juveniles;
- retreating while watching the party.

The GM describes the signals rather than simply saying "it is aggressive."

Checks such as Perception, Insight, Nature, Survival or Animal Handling may reveal more when appropriate under Pokémon 5e/D&D rules.

A failed check does not erase obvious physical evidence.

Escalation should follow cause and effect:

**notice -> warning -> threat -> attack -> pursuit/disengagement**

when that sequence fits the species and circumstances.

Ambush predators, panic, anomalies and already-hostile creatures may skip stages when fiction supports it.

---

## 9. Cooperation with humans

Wild Pokémon may work with humans without being captured.

Examples:

- guide travelers;
- lead them to water/shelter;
- request help for an injured group member;
- cooperate against a shared predator;
- warn of danger;
- help move an obstacle;
- temporarily defend a camp;
- trade access/food for assistance;
- follow the party for curiosity or safety;
- form a recurring relationship.

Cooperation does **not** automatically mean obedience, ownership or willingness to enter a Poké Ball.

A recurring wild Pokémon may remember treatment and behave differently in later encounters.

---

## 10. Capture is not the default resolution

Possible successful resolutions include:

- peaceful passage;
- avoidance;
- observation;
- feeding;
- de-escalation;
- rescue;
- negotiation through behavior;
- cooperation;
- driving the creature away;
- winning territory/resource access;
- combat victory;
- capture;
- forming trust for a later encounter.

A Pokémon may have strong fictional reasons not to leave its family, territory, young or social group.

Apply exact Pokémon 5e capture legality and mechanics. Do not replace legal capture rules with narration alone.

---

## 11. D&D-style scripted encounter scenes

Random does not mean context-free.

After species selection, frame a **small encounter scene** rather than spawning a static target.

Useful scene archetypes:

- predator pursuing prey;
- family at a water source;
- territorial warning;
- pack crossing a route;
- injured Pokémon hiding;
- juvenile separated from group;
- Alpha/Beta dominance dispute;
- Pokémon feeding from a carcass;
- migration blocking travel;
- creature stealing supplies;
- wild Pokémon trapped by terrain/humans;
- group fleeing a larger threat;
- curious Pokémon shadowing the party;
- two species competing over resources;
- cooperative Pokémon seeking assistance;
- sleeping/resting wildlife accidentally disturbed.

The GM may roll among fitting archetypes or choose one when world state clearly implies it.

Never choose an archetype that contradicts species ecology merely for drama.

---

## 12. Information discipline

Do not expose hidden labels such as:

- disposition = territorial;
- lethal_intent = true;
- alpha = true;
- encounter archetype;
- escalation score.

Show evidence in fiction.

Example:

> The Ursaring does not charge. It plants itself between you and the brush, shoulders high, claws digging into the soil. Two smaller shapes move behind it.

The player decides how to interpret and respond.

---

## 13. Persistence

Persist only encounters with meaningful future consequences.

Examples:

- an Alpha was defeated, spared or killed;
- a pack was driven from territory;
- a family was rescued;
- a wild Pokémon was repeatedly fed;
- a population became hostile toward humans;
- a predator migrated;
- a local species was over-captured;
- a recurring Pokémon formed trust with the party;
- poaching or ranger intervention changed behavior.

World-state should remember the consequence rather than rerolling the location as if nothing happened.

---

## 14. GM non-negotiables

- Wild Pokémon are creatures first, encounter content second.
- Encounter != combat.
- Combat != fight to the death.
- Dangerous != automatically hostile.
- Alpha != automatically aggressive or mechanically buffed.
- Packs/families/colonies must behave socially.
- Wild Pokémon can attack humans when justified.
- Wild Pokémon can attempt lethal violence when justified.
- Wild Pokémon can cooperate with humans without capture.
- The player is never forced to fight, capture, spare, flee or befriend a Pokémon.
- Ecology does not scale itself to protect the player.
- Exact Pokémon 5e mechanics remain authoritative.
- Use **FICTION -> MECHANICS -> FICTION** throughout.
